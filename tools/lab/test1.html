Caesar Cipher

A classical substitution cipher that shifts each letter by a fixed number of positions in the alphabet. Attributed to Julius Caesar, it is one of the simplest and most widely known encryption techniques.

Ctrl + Enter to process
Ctrl + Shift + C Copy

The Caesar Cipher is a monoalphabetic substitution cipher where each letter in the plaintext is replaced by a letter some fixed number of positions down the alphabet. For example, with a right shift of 3, A becomes D, B becomes E, and so on. The cipher wraps around the end of the alphabet, so X becomes A.

Despite its historical significance, the Caesar Cipher offers virtually no security by modern standards. With only 25 possible keys (excluding the identity shift of 0), it can be broken instantly via brute force.

Encryption:

E(x) = (x + k) mod 26

Decryption:

D(y) = (y - k) mod 26

Where x is the plaintext letter index (A=0, B=1, …), k is the shift key, and y is the ciphertext letter index.

Encrypting "HELLO" with a shift of 3:

Plaintext H E L L O
Index (0–25) 7 4 11 11 14
Shifted Index 10 7 14 14 17
Ciphertext K H O O R

Result: HELLO → KHOOR

  • The cipher is named after Julius Caesar, who used a shift of 3 for military communications.
  • ROT13 is a special case of the Caesar Cipher with a shift of 13; it is its own inverse.
  • The cipher preserves letter frequencies, making it vulnerable to frequency analysis.
  • This tool supports both standard Latin alphabet and extended character sets.
